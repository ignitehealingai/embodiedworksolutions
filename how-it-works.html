The real concerns about AI

Do I have to use AI?

No. Keeping the human in AI includes deciding where it doesn’t belong. Sometimes the best answer is a simpler tool, or no tool at all. You’re always the one deciding.

Why learn AI at all?

Because it’s becoming the new Google. When search was new, people had real fears about it—and some of them were right. Now “Did you google it?” is the first question we ask. AI is moving the same way. It has real problems and real benefits. Understanding how it works is how you get a say, in your own life and in the bigger conversation.

I already have an idea. Can you help me grow it with AI?

Yes—that’s half of what I do. We’ll use AI to research, draft, plan, or build, and you stay the one making the calls.

What happens to what I type into AI?

With most commercial tools, what you type may be kept or used to train future models. So I won’t promise “safe.” What I will do: show you how to check what a tool keeps, help you decide what you’re willing to offer and what should stay yours, and point you to tools that run on your own computer when the work is tender or unfinished. And I always ask before putting your information into an AI tool. The question isn’t whether these tools watch us. It’s whether we’re awake while we use them.

Can I trust what it tells me?

Not blindly. AI can be wrong and sound completely sure, and it isn’t good at knowing when to stop. You’ll practice checking it, so the judgment about how much to trust it stays with you.

Won’t it make everyone think the same way?

That risk is real. When one model answers millions of questions, it pulls toward the most common answer. The antidote isn’t refusing the tool. It’s noticing when you’re using it to confirm what you already think instead of to question it—and bringing your own judgment into the conversation, not handing it over.

What about bias?

Bias in AI isn’t a future problem, and it isn’t a glitch. It reflects whose stories got recorded and whose didn’t. We’ll practice noticing where a tool flattens or misses people, and pushing back when it does.

Will it make me worse at thinking or writing?

It can, if you use it to avoid thinking instead of to sharpen it. We use it to support your judgment and keep your own voice in what you make.

What about the climate cost?

It’s real, and if you’re paying attention to it, good. Using AI with care includes deciding where it’s worth the cost and where it isn’t. Opting out entirely doesn’t shrink the footprint; it removes your voice from the conversation about what it should be.

Is AI going to take jobs?

Some, yes—and not equally. I won’t smooth that over. What helps is learning the tools with intention and leaning into what only people can do: read a room, catch what the model missed, and make judgment calls that depend on the whole human context.

I’m skeptical of AI. Is this for me?

Yes. Especially you. The future needs more than people who adopt AI—it needs accountability. The people who feel what’s wrong before they can name it are exactly who should be in the room.

I wrote more about all of this in Before You Decide You’re Against AI.
